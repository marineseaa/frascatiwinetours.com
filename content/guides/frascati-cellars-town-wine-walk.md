Frascati is built on soft volcanic tufa full of old cellars. There is no regular, ticketed "underground Frascati" tour, but you can still get into the grottoes: eat or drink in a restaurant set inside a tufa cellar in the old town, book a visit to a Roman-era wine cave at an estate just outside town, or watch for the occasional open day. The easiest way to see it all is on foot. A self-guided walk of two to four hours takes you from the view of Rome on Piazza Marconi past Villa Aldobrandini, the cathedral and the market square to a cantina for a jug of house wine, and you finish with dinner in a cellar.

## What's under Frascati?

Tufa, and plenty of holes in it. Frascati sits on the volcanic Alban Hills (Colli Albani). The soft tufa is easy to dig and stays cool and humid, so for centuries locals carved out cellars to store and age wine. Many of these grottoes run under the old town, and many are still used as wine cellars and storerooms.

Older than the cellars are the Roman water cisterns of the Tusculum area, cut into the same rock to hold water for the villas on these slopes. Cavers have been mapping the cavities under the town, but there is no public tour of them.

## The self-guided Frascati wine walk

*Times are approximate. Walk at your own pace, and check opening days on the day.*

### Stop 1: Frascati station → Piazza Marconi (5–10 min)

Climb the stairs from the station up to Piazza Marconi, the town's front door. It's steep but short. At the top you'll find the town hall, a war memorial and the old Aldobrandini stables.

**The view:** cross to the terrace promenade looking back over the Roman countryside. On clear days you can see the dome of St Peter's on the horizon. Grab a coffee on the square; this is also your best toilet stop.

### Stop 2: Villa Aldobrandini gardens (45–60 min; limited opening days)

The grand villa above the square was built from 1598 for Cardinal Pietro Aldobrandini, nephew of Pope Clement VIII. The palace is private and still a family home. The gardens open to visitors on limited days only, and the hours are not published. Ask at the tourist office on the square in the morning, before lunch. If you get in, look for the water theatre, with Atlas holding up the world in the central niche.

### Stop 3: Scuderie Aldobrandini – Museo Tuscolano (30–45 min; closed Mondays)

The villa's 17th-century stables, redesigned by the architect Massimiliano Fuksas, hold finds from ancient Tusculum and Frascati, including pieces linked to Dionysus, the god of wine. The museum's own leaflet gives Tuesday to Sunday, 10:00 to 19:00, with a €3 full ticket. This is your rainy-day stop.

### Stop 4: Piazza San Pietro and the cathedral (20 min)

A short walk from Piazza Marconi. The Baroque façade of the Cathedral of San Pietro survived the bombing of 8 September 1943, which destroyed much of the town. Inside, look for the memorial to Charles Edward Stuart, "Bonnie Prince Charlie", a surprising find for British visitors. A Baroque fountain fills the square.

### Stop 5: Chiesa del Gesù → Piazza San Rocco (15 min)

Pop into the Chiesa del Gesù, a Jesuit church with illusionist painting by Andrea Pozzo, including a painted dome on a flat ceiling. Then wander through the lanes to Piazza San Rocco and the Bishop's Palace. Make a note of Cantina Simonetti for later.

### Stop 6: Piazza del Mercato (20 min)

The market square is your picnic shop:
- **Porchetta and bread** from the shops and stalls around the square
- A ***pupazza frascatana*** from one of the pastry shops: the honey biscuit shaped like a woman with three breasts, "two for milk, one for wine". Many shops close for a long midday break.

### Stop 7: A cantina where you bring your own food (45–60 min)

Take your porchetta to an old-style fraschetta, where you buy only the family's wine, by the jug, and eat what you brought. A few of these still survive in the lanes around the market, with limited opening days that change. Most fraschette in town now serve their own food and wine, so ask before unwrapping yours.

### Stop 8: Sunset and a cellar dinner

Head back to Piazza Marconi or Villa Torlonia park for the sunset view. Then go underground for dinner at Cantina Simonetti or ConTatto, or eat at a family fraschetta such as Osteria Fraschetta Trinca on Via Balilla.

> **Last train:** check the last direct train back to Roma Termini in the Trenitalia app for your date before you order a second carafe. It changes with the day of the week and the timetable. Our [Rome to Frascati by train](/guides/rome-to-frascati-by-train/) guide covers tickets and the journey.

## Route variants

### At the weekend

If Villa Aldobrandini's gardens are closed on your day, swap Stop 2 for Villa Torlonia park and its Teatro delle Acque, a Baroque water theatre that survived the 1943 bombing. The park is a free public garden with a gentle loop path.

### Add Tusculum (mainly Sundays)

High above Frascati lie the ruins of ancient Tusculum, with a Roman theatre, a forum and wide views. You have two options:
- **Hike up:** a waymarked trail climbs from the edge of town in about two hours, with no refreshments on the way. Bring water.
- **Taxi up:** then a short walk to the ruins.

The archaeological park opens mainly on Sundays, with longer hours from May to September, and published schedules disagree. Check tuscolo.org or call before you go.

### In summer heat

Walk in the morning, then retreat to a cellar lunch. The tufa grottoes stay cool even in midsummer.

### On a rainy day

Museum, cathedral, Gesù, then a long, slow lunch in a cellar.

### Add a winery visit

Book a tasting at an estate such as Tenuta di Pietra Porzia or Castel de Paolis, then take a taxi from Piazza Marconi. Book your return taxi too. Or let someone else handle it: several half-day tours combine the town with a winery and lunch, compared in our guide to [choosing a Frascati wine tour](/guides/choose-frascati-wine-tour/).

### Accessibility

Frascati is steep, with stairs and cobbles, and the walk from the station involves a staircase. Villa Torlonia park is the gentlest green space. The Tusculum trail is not suitable for wheelchairs or buggies. For step-free access, consider a [private tour](/private-frascati-wine-tours/) with door-to-door transport.

## A Frascati wine crawl: fraschetta to fraschetta

Prefer to skip the sights? Here's the short version:

1. **Piazza del Mercato:** porchetta sandwich
2. **A BYO cantina:** a jug of house wine with your sandwich
3. **A wine bar or enoteca:** a glass of labelled Frascati Superiore DOCG (carafe wine is generic house wine, not necessarily the DOC)
4. **Pasticceria:** a *pupazza* and, if you can find it, a glass of sweet Cannellino
5. **A cellar restaurant:** dinner underground

> **Pace yourself:** fraschetta wine comes by the jug. Drink water and eat as you go, and leave time for the walk back down the stairs to the station.

## A little history: the bombing of 1943

On 8 September 1943, the day Italy's armistice was announced, Allied bombers struck Frascati, then the seat of the German command in southern Italy. Much of the town was destroyed and hundreds of civilians were killed. Villa Torlonia and the railway station were destroyed; the cathedral's façade survived. Frascati commemorates the raid every year on 8 September.

When you walk the old town, much of what you see was rebuilt after the war.

## Practical tips

- **Go Tuesday to Friday** for the museum and your best chance at Villa Aldobrandini's gardens.
- **Wear comfortable, grippy shoes**: stairs, slopes and cobbles.
- **Bring a layer** for cellar dinners; tufa grottoes stay cool all year.
- **Carry some cash** for small shops and fraschette.

## FAQ

### Can you visit the underground cellars in Frascati?

Yes, but not on a regular public tour. You can eat or drink in restaurants built into tufa cellars in the old town, book a cellar visit at a nearby wine estate, or join occasional open-day events.

### What are Frascati's grotte?

Cellars, tunnels and cisterns dug into the soft volcanic tufa beneath Frascati and its surroundings. Many were used, and some still are, to store and age wine, cheese and cured meats. Some date to Roman times.

### Is there a wine cave near Rome you can visit?

Yes. Estates just outside Frascati, about 30 minutes from Rome by train plus a short taxi ride, offer tastings in tufa galleries. Tenuta di Pietra Porzia's tunnels are about 2,000 years old. Book ahead.

### How long does it take to walk around Frascati?

The old town can be seen in 2–3 hours. Allow 4 hours or more with a long lunch, a cellar dinner, or a visit to Villa Aldobrandini's gardens.

### Is Villa Aldobrandini open to visitors?

The gardens open on limited days, and the palace is private. Hours are not published, so ask at the tourist office on Piazza Marconi.

### What is a fraschetta?

A traditional wine tavern in the Castelli Romani hills. Customers once brought their own food to eat with the house wine. Most now serve porchetta, cured meats, cheese and Roman pastas, and a few still let you bring your own food.

### Can I bring my own food to a cantina in Frascati?

At a few traditional places, yes. Most fraschette now have their own menu, so always ask first.

### Is Frascati walkable?

Yes, but it's hilly. Expect stairs from the station and sloping, cobbled streets.

### What can I do in Frascati on a Sunday?

Visit Villa Torlonia park, the cathedral and the Scuderie Aldobrandini museum, which opens Tuesday to Sunday. Sunday is also the main opening day of the Tusculum archaeological park above town.

### What time is the last train from Frascati to Rome?

It varies by date and day of the week. Check the Trenitalia app for your travel day.
