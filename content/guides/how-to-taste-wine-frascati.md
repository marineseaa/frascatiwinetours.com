To taste wine, look at it against something white, swirl it, smell it, take a small sip and let it move around your mouth, then notice how long the flavour lasts. Then decide whether you like it. Frascati is a good wine to learn on: the same volcanic hills and the same Malvasia grapes give sparkling, dry, aged and sweet styles, and most estates also pour a local red.
<!--more-->

## Tasting wine in Frascati at a glance

One Frascati cellar, a handful of styles and about ten Italian words cover most of what a beginner needs. You can practise all of it on a half-day tasting near Rome, or at home with three bottles.

- **The method:** look, swirl, smell, sip, finish, then decide if you like it
- **The order:** sparkling, light dry white, fuller white, red, sweet
- **The one Italian word to learn:** *sapido* (savoury, slightly salty), a word in the official description of Frascati Superiore
- **The surprise:** in Italian tastings, *fresco* means lively acidity, not "cold"
- **You can spit.** It is normal and polite.
- **No expertise required.** "Peachy, floral, fresh, almond finish" is a perfectly good tasting note.

## How do you taste wine? The 5-step method

This is a beginner's version of the approach the big wine schools teach, including the Wine & Spirit Education Trust (WSET) and the Italian Sommelier Association (AIS). All of them work through the same three stages: sight, smell and taste.

| Step | What to do | What to notice | Frascati example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Look** | Tilt the glass over a white napkin or tablecloth | Is it clear? What colour? How deep? | Pale straw for young Frascati; deeper straw to gold for Riserva and the sweet Cannellino |
| **2. Swirl** | Two or three small circles with the base on the table | Releases the aromas into the glass | Watch the "legs" run down the glass (see the questions below) |
| **3. Smell** | One short sniff, pause, then another | Fruit? Flowers? Something else? | White flowers, apple and pear, white peach, citrus, herbs such as sage |
| **4. Sip** | A small sip; hold it, move it around, draw in a little air | Sweetness, acidity, body, alcohol (and tannin in reds) | Dry, fresh, softly rounded, with a savoury, slightly salty tang |
| **5. Finish** | Swallow or spit, then count the seconds the flavour lasts | How long does it linger? What is the last note? | Guides often describe a faint bitter-almond echo |

**Then ask yourself one question: do I like it?** Professionals reach the same conclusion, with more vocabulary.

### Why do you swirl wine?

Swirling mixes the wine with air, so the aromas reach your nose more easily. It is also why tasting pours are small: you need room in the glass. Fill it no more than about a third full.

### Why does smell matter so much?

Most of what we call taste is smell. As you sip, aromas travel from the back of your mouth up to your nose (retronasal smell). That is why food tastes of so little when you have a cold.

### A trick for describing aromas

Before you sip, name two fruits and two other things you can smell. For a Frascati Superiore that might be "pear and white peach; white flowers and something stony". You have just written a tasting note.

## How do you describe what you're tasting?

You do not need fancy words. Use these simple tests:

| Element | Plain-English test | Frascati example |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweetness** | Can you feel sugar on the tip of your tongue? | Superiore: none (dry). Cannellino: clearly sweet. |
| **Acidity** | Does your mouth water after you swallow? | Medium and refreshing: *fresco* |
| **Body** | Does it feel like skimmed, semi-skimmed or whole milk? | Light to medium; Riserva is fuller |
| **Alcohol** | Is there warmth at the back of your throat? | Moderate |
| **Tannin** (reds) | Does it dry and grip your gums, like strong tea? | The local red Cesanese: moderate, gentle |
| **Finish** | How many seconds does the flavour last? | A lingering bitter-almond note |

A complete beginner's tasting note can be as short as this:

> *"Pale straw. Pear, white flowers and a hint of sage. Dry, fresh, softly salty, with an almond finish. Lovely with salty food."*

## Why is Frascati good for learning to taste?

One small area gives you almost every style, from the same grapes on the same volcanic soil. In a single tasting you can compare:

| Wine | What it teaches you |
|---|---|
| **Frascati Spumante DOC** | Bubbles: texture, freshness, how fizz lifts flavour |
| **Frascati DOC** | The baseline: a light, fresh, delicately floral white |
| **Frascati Superiore DOCG** | *Sapidità*: that savoury, saline edge, plus more intensity |
| **Frascati Superiore Riserva DOCG** | Age (and sometimes a hint of wood): deeper colour, rounder body, honeyed and nutty notes |
| **A Cesanese red** | Tannin and spice: a gentle first lesson in reds, with cherry and pepper |
| **Cannellino di Frascati DOCG** | Sweetness and balance: how acidity keeps a sweet wine fresh |

Many Frascati tours pour three of these, typically a Superiore, a red and the Cannellino. That is a short course in itself.

> **Learning on a volcano:** Frascati sits on the slopes of the Alban Hills (Colli Albani), a volcanic complex that has not erupted for tens of thousands of years and is classed as quiescent. The vineyards grow on volcanic soils, which locals credit for Frascati's savoury, mineral feel.

## What does each Frascati wine taste like? The official descriptions

Italy's [production rules](/guides/frascati-wine-guide/) (*disciplinari*) for the Frascati DOCGs include official tasting descriptions. They help beginners, because they tell you what you are meant to find.

| Wine | Colour (official) | Aroma (official) | Palate (official) | Sweetness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Frascati Superiore DOCG** | *Giallo paglierino più o meno intenso*: straw yellow, more or less intense | *Intenso, con profumo caratteristico delicato*: intense, with a delicate characteristic scent | *Secco, sapido, morbido, fine, vellutato*: dry, savoury, soft, refined, velvety | Dry |
| **Frascati Superiore Riserva DOCG** | As Superiore | As Superiore; wine kept in wood may show a light trace of it | As Superiore, aged at least 12 months from 1 November of the harvest year, three of them in bottle | Dry |
| **Cannellino di Frascati DOCG** | *Giallo paglierino intenso*: deep straw yellow | *Caratteristico, fine, delicato*: characteristic, refined, delicate | *Fruttato, caratteristico*: fruity, characteristic | Sweet, with at least 35 g of residual sugar per litre |

Look at the Superiore description again: ***secco, sapido, morbido, fine, vellutato***. Five Italian words make a complete tasting note. Learn them and you will follow most of what a Frascati winemaker says. The plain Frascati DOC and the Spumante sit below these in the hierarchy: lighter, simpler wines that make a useful baseline.

### How do the grapes change the taste?

Frascati is at least 70% Malvasia, either Malvasia Bianca di Candia, Malvasia del Lazio or both. Which Malvasia dominates matters:

| Grape | What it brings | In the glass |
|---|---|---|
| **Malvasia del Lazio (Malvasia Puntinata)** | Yellow fruit such as peach, plus sage; considered the higher-quality local grape | Riper, more perfumed, fuller |
| **Malvasia Bianca di Candia** | Light body, high acidity; white flowers, citrus, apple, herbs | Crisper, leaner, more neutral |
| **Bellone, Bombino, Greco, Trebbiano** | Supporting grapes (up to 30%) | Freshness, structure |

Producers who favour Malvasia Puntinata tend to make richer, more aromatic Frascati. Ask your host what is in the blend.

### What to look for in a good Frascati

Professional tasters who write about the best Frascati Superiore and Riserva bottles keep coming back to the same word: saline. That is *sapido* in English. In a good glass you will usually find white peach or other stone fruit, fresh herbs, a stony edge and a slightly salty finish that makes you want the next sip. A Riserva adds a little honey and more weight.

## The 10 Italian tasting words you'll hear in a Frascati cellar

Learn these and you will follow almost any Italian tasting:

| Italian | Say it | English | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **secco** | SEK-ko | dry | No noticeable sugar |
| **abboccato** | ab-bok-KAH-to | off-dry | Just a touch of sweetness |
| **amabile** | ah-MAH-bee-leh | medium-sweet | Clearly sweet, not lush |
| **dolce** | DOHL-cheh | sweet | Dessert wine |
| **fresco** | FRES-ko | fresh | Lively acidity, not temperature |
| **sapido** | SAH-pee-doh | savoury | That salty, mineral tang |
| **morbido** | MOR-bee-doh | soft | Smooth, rounded |
| **caldo** | KAHL-doh | warm | You can feel the alcohol |
| **equilibrato** | eh-kwee-lee-BRAH-toh | balanced | Nothing sticks out |
| **persistente** | per-sees-TEN-teh | long | The flavour lingers |

> **The false friend that catches everyone:** when an Italian sommelier calls a wine *fresco*, they mean its acidity. A *fresco* wine makes your mouth water, whatever its temperature. In the same way, *caldo* ("warm") describes alcohol. Neither word is about how the wine was served.

### How Italian sommeliers think about balance

The Italian Sommelier Association (AIS) teaches a simple idea: every wine balances soft elements (*morbidezze*: sugar, alcohol and smoothness) against hard elements (*durezze*: acidity, tannin and that savoury, salty *sapidità*). A wine is *equilibrato* (balanced) when neither side dominates. Cannellino is a good example: acidity keeps its sweetness fresh.

## What order should you taste wines in?

Go from light to heavy and from dry to sweet. Sugar coats the palate, and a dry wine tasted after a sweet one can seem sharp or bitter.

On a Frascati tasting, that means:

1. **Spumante** (sparkling)
2. **Frascati DOC** (light dry white)
3. **Frascati Superiore** (fuller dry white)
4. **Superiore Riserva** (aged white)
5. **Cesanese** (red)
6. **Cannellino** (sweet)

## What temperature should Frascati be served at?

Very cold wine tastes of little, and warm wine tastes flabby and alcoholic. These are our recommendations, based on standard serving ranges:

| Wine | Serve at |
|---|---|
| Frascati DOC / Spumante | 8–10°C (well chilled) |
| Frascati Superiore | 10–12°C (chilled) |
| Superiore Riserva | 12–14°C (lightly chilled) |
| Cannellino | 8–12°C (chilled) |
| Cesanese (red) | 14–16°C (lightly cool) |

**At home:** take whites out of the fridge 10–15 minutes before serving. Give light reds 20 minutes in the fridge.

## How does food change the way wine tastes?

A lot, which is why Frascati tastings come with snacks.

- **Salt and fat** (porchetta, pecorino, salumi) soften a wine's acidity and bitterness, so it tastes rounder.
- **Sweet food** makes a dry wine taste sharper and more sour. That is why hosts keep the sweet Cannellino for *ciambelline al vino* (wine biscuits) and *tozzetti* (almond biscuits).
- **The local classic:** Cannellino with salty pecorino romano, a sweet-and-salty pairing local producers recommend.
- **Plain bread and water** reset your palate between wines.

## How do you taste olive oil?

Many Frascati tastings include the estate's extra-virgin olive oil. Here is how to taste it:

1. **Warm** a small cup of oil in your hand.
2. **Sniff** it: you are looking for fresh, green, fruity aromas such as cut grass, tomato leaf or artichoke.
3. **Sip** a little and suck in some air over it, slurping as you do.
4. Notice the **bitterness** at the back of your tongue and a **peppery catch** in your throat.

Under International Olive Council tasting standards, fruitiness, bitterness and pungency are all positive qualities. If the oil makes you cough, that is a compliment. Fresh, high-quality oil often does, especially the new-season *olio nuovo* in late autumn.

## How can you tell if a wine is faulty?

Faulty wine is uncommon, but here is what to look for:

| Fault | Smells like | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Corked (TCA)** | Damp cardboard, musty cellar, wet dog | A chemical taint, usually from the cork; it dulls the fruit |
| **Oxidised** | Bruised apple, sherry; browning colour | Too much air exposure |
| **Reduced** | Struck match, rubber, rotten egg | Often fades with air; give it a swirl |

**Not a fault:** bits of cork floating in the glass, or harmless tartrate crystals in the bottom of the bottle.

## Beginner questions, answered

### What are wine legs (or tears)?

They are the streaks that run down the inside of the glass after you swirl. Alcohol evaporating faster than water causes them. More pronounced legs suggest more alcohol or sugar. They say nothing about quality.

### Do I have to swirl?

No, but it helps the aromas reach your nose. Keep the base on the table if you are nervous about spills.

### Is sweet wine unsophisticated?

Not at all. Cannellino has its own DOCG, Italy's top quality category. What matters in a sweet wine is balance: enough acidity to stop it tasting cloying.

### Why spit?

So you can taste several wines with a clear head. It is completely normal at tastings. Ask for a spittoon, a *sputacchiera* in Italian.

### How many wines can I taste before my palate gets tired?

There is no fixed number. Tannic reds and high-alcohol wines tire your palate faster. The usual three-wine Frascati tasting, with water and bread, is very manageable.

### How do I describe a wine without sounding pretentious?

Use the two-fruits-and-two-other-aromas trick plus one structure word. "Peachy, floral, fresh, with an almond finish" is a perfectly good note.

### What does "minerality" mean?

It is a debated term, but people use it for a stony, salty or flinty impression, much like the Italian *sapido*. Frascati, grown on volcanic soil, is often described this way.

### Is Frascati just cheap jug wine?

Its reputation suffered from decades of bulk production. Top Frascati Superiore and Riserva bottles now get high scores from respected critics, and good examples can age well for several years.

## Try it at home: a three-bottle Frascati tasting

You do not need to be in Italy. Buy three bottles and follow along:

1. **A Frascati DOC**: your baseline. Light, fresh and floral.
2. **A Frascati Superiore DOCG**, ideally one made with Malvasia Puntinata: notice the extra intensity and that salty edge.
3. **A Cannellino di Frascati DOCG** (usually in half bottles): taste it with an almond biscuit or a sliver of pecorino.

Serve each at the temperatures above, taste in that order, and fill in a tasting card for each one.

### Frascati vs other popular whites

| | **Frascati** | **Pinot Grigio** | **Sauvignon Blanc** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aromas** | White flowers, pear, peach, herbs | Neutral, lemon, green apple | Pungent: grass, gooseberry, green pepper, passion fruit |
| **Texture** | Soft, savoury, slightly salty | Light, crisp | Zesty, sharp |
| **Finish** | Bitter almond | Clean, short | Tangy |
| **Try it if you like** | Pinot Grigio but want more character | Easy, neutral whites | Bold, aromatic whites |

## FAQ

### What are the steps to tasting wine?

Look at the colour and clarity, swirl to release the aromas, smell, sip slowly so the wine coats your mouth, then notice the finish. Finally, decide whether you like it.

### What should I look for when tasting white wine?

Colour (pale to golden), aromas (fruit, flowers, herbs), sweetness, acidity (does your mouth water?), body and the length of the finish.

### What does Frascati wine taste like?

Typically dry and fresh, with white flowers, pear, apple and white peach, a savoury, slightly salty edge and a gentle bitter-almond finish. Cannellino is the sweet version.

### What does *sapido* mean in wine?

*Sapido* is the Italian tasting term for a savoury, slightly salty or mineral sensation on the palate. It is one of the official descriptors of Frascati Superiore DOCG.

### What does *fresco* mean in wine tasting?

In Italian tasting, *fresco* describes lively acidity, the mouth-watering freshness of a wine, not its temperature.

### What order should you taste wine in?

From light to heavy and dry to sweet: sparkling, then light whites, fuller whites, reds, and sweet wines last.

### Is it rude to spit at a wine tasting?

No. Spitting is normal and expected at tastings, especially if you are trying several wines.

### Do I need to know about wine to go on a Frascati tasting?

Not at all. Many guests are beginners, and hosts explain each wine as they pour.

### At what temperature should Frascati be served?

Well chilled for everyday Frascati (around 8–10°C), slightly warmer for Superiore (10–12°C) and Riserva (12–14°C). Cannellino is best chilled.

### How do you taste olive oil?

Warm it in a small cup, smell it, sip it while drawing in air, then notice the fruitiness, the bitterness on your tongue and the peppery catch in your throat. A cough means it is fresh.
